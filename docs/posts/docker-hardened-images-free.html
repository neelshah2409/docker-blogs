Docker Hardened Images Go Free: What 1,000+ Hardened Images Means for Your Supply Chain

A catalog that used to sit behind an enterprise contract is now a docker pull away. That changes the calculus on which base image is the "default" choice for a new Dockerfile — but migrating an existing one takes a bit more than a one-line swap.

Estimated time 11 min Format applied comparison Since Dec 2025
what changed

1,000+ images, free, Apache 2.0

Docker Hardened Images (DHI) launched in May 2025 as a commercial catalog. In December 2025, Docker made the entire catalog — more than 1,000 images built on Debian and Alpine, covering runtimes, frameworks, databases, and infrastructure tooling — free and open source under Apache 2.0, available through dhi.io and Docker Hub.

For most teams outside regulated industries, the free tier is the meaningful change — everything that made these images worth using is no longer paywalled, and only the compliance guarantees and SLAs stay commercial.

why it matters

Why the base image is where CVE exposure actually starts

A standard base image typically ships a full OS userland — a shell, a package manager, compilers, debugging tools — most of which your application never touches at runtime but which all still count toward your attack surface and CVE count. DHI images are distroless-style: no shell, no package manager, non-root by default, stripped to what the runtime actually needs.

Distroless

No shell, no package manager, minimal OS layer — there's very little left in the image for an attacker to repurpose even after gaining code execution.

Attack surface

Every binary and library present in an image, whether your app uses it or not — fewer packages means fewer places a CVE can land.

Provenance

A verifiable record of how an image was built (SLSA Build Level 3) — proof it's what it claims to be, not just a scan result you have to trust.

The exact package and size deltas vary by image and are worth checking for your specific base, not treated as a universal guarantee — but the direction and rough magnitude hold consistently across the catalog: fewer packages, smaller images, materially fewer places for a known CVE to exist in the first place.

      worth keeping in mind
      

        A fair caveat
        
Some developers have greeted the free tier with justified caution rather than pure enthusiasm, pointing to Docker's own history of narrowing free offerings — Docker Hub pull-rate limits, and pricing changes to Docker Desktop's free tier, among them. None of that means DHI won't stay free; it means treating "free forever" as a current fact rather than a permanent guarantee is the more defensible position, the same way it would be for any vendor's free tier.

      

    


    
    

      the fuller picture
      
Where this fits alongside Docker AI Governance

      
Hardened images and AI Governance solve adjacent but distinct halves of a supply-chain story. DHI is about the artifact itself — is this specific image's provenance verifiable, and is its attack surface actually minimal. Governance is about behavior — which images, registries, and tools an agent or developer is even allowed to reach in the first place.

      
Put together, an organization can go a step further than just adopting hardened images individually: a centrally defined Governance policy can require that only images from the hardened catalog are pullable at all, so the "use the hardened base" decision stops being something every developer or agent has to remember on their own, and becomes something the platform simply enforces.
