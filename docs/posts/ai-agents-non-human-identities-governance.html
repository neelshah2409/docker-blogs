AI Agents Are Non-Human Identities Now — Is Your Governance Model Ready?

An agent that can initiate its own tool calls, at machine speed, unattended, isn't a script and isn't a user. It's a third category most identity programs never built a lane for — and the gap is showing.

Estimated time 13 min Category perspective Audience platform & security
the trend

Agents that act, not just agents that answer

A year ago, most "AI in the loop" meant a model producing text a person then acted on. That's no longer the typical shape. Agents now call APIs, open pull requests, provision infrastructure, and query databases directly — continuously, at whatever pace the task requires, and largely outside the moment-by-moment visibility a human reviewer used to provide. The action isn't proposed anymore in most of these flows. It's taken.

That shift is exactly what turns "the agent" from a feature of your software into an identity your organization has to account for — something that authenticates, holds permissions, and acts on systems, the same three things true of any user or service account, just at a completely different tempo.

Non-human identity (NHI)

Any account or credential that authenticates and acts without a person directly driving each action — service accounts, bots, and now autonomous agents.

Machine-speed action

Volume and pace no human review cadence was designed for — hundreds of tool calls in the time a quarterly access review covers one line item.

Visibility gap

The distance between what an agent is authorized to do and what anyone can currently see it actually doing, in real time.

analysis

Why identity and access management wasn't built for this

Traditional IAM has two well-worn shapes: a human identity, reviewed periodically, with access a person requests and a manager approves; and a static service account, provisioned once for a specific integration and rarely touched again. Both assume the actor's behavior is either slow enough for a human to review or narrow enough that its scope barely needs revisiting.

An agent breaks both assumptions simultaneously — it acts far faster than a human review cycle, but its behavior is broad and improvisational in a way a fixed-scope service account never was.

what governing an agent actually means

Three things a real agent-governance model requires

1
Scoped credentials
Not "the same API key the team has always used" — a credential narrowed to the specific task and session, ideally short-lived enough that a leaked one is worthless shortly after.
2
Audit trails generated at the point of decision
Not reconstructed from what an agent produced afterward — a log collector can see what an agent did, but nothing about what it tried to do and was stopped from doing, because a blocked action leaves no output to collect.
3
Revocability
The ability to cut off a specific agent identity's access immediately, without taking down the service account sixteen other things also depend on.

common misconception
What people assume

"We already scope our service accounts down — an agent using one of those is basically already governed."

What's actually true

Scope alone answers "what could this identity theoretically do," not "what did it actually do today, and can we tell the difference between it acting normally and acting compromised." A quarterly review of a service account's permissions was never designed to catch an agent that made ten thousand decisions since the last time anyone looked. The gap isn't how narrow the grant is — it's that nothing is watching in the window between reviews, and an agent's whole operating tempo lives inside that window.

one industry response

Streaming policy decisions to the SIEM, not just outputs

Docker's recent addition to AI Governance is a useful concrete example of what "governed" starts to look like in practice. Every policy evaluation an agent triggers — allowed, denied, or held for a human — generates a structured record at the moment the decision is made, and that record streams directly into whatever SIEM a security team already runs, alongside a searchable 90-day history.

The detail that matters most isn't the SIEM integration itself, familiar from a dozen other tools — it's capturing all three outcomes, not just the successful ones. A record of what an agent was allowed to do shows it's active. A record of what it was denied is the only way to know whether the controls around it are doing anything at all, and that record can only be produced at the enforcement point itself, never assembled afterward from logs of what the agent actually output.

implications for platform and security teams

What to actually change

Treating agents as a distinct identity class, not an extension of an existing service account program, changes a handful of concrete decisions:

1
Give agents their own identity lifecycle
Provisioned, scoped, and retired independently of any human user or shared service account they happen to run alongside.
2
Move review cadence from periodic to continuous
A quarterly access review can't catch a pattern that formed and caused damage inside a single afternoon.
3
Design for revocation, not just detection
A governance model that can only tell you something went wrong, without a fast path to cut that specific identity off, is half a control.
4
Route agent policy events into existing security tooling
A separate dashboard no one checks is worse than no dashboard — the events need to land where the SOC is already looking.
